\noindent
The \texttt{KernelDensityEstimator} Kotlin class converts a stream of
Geiger--Müller counts into a continuous dose-rate curve
$\hat\lambda(t)$ in $\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}$, together with Garwood Poisson
confidence intervals based on an effective count and exposure.  The original
fixed-bandwidth estimator and the optional adaptive estimator use separate
calculation paths.  Garwood intervals are exact for integer Poisson counts;
their effective-count application to a smoothed KDE is an approximation.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Epanechnikov Kernel}

The class uses the \emph{Epanechnikov} kernel with bandwidth $\h > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  K_\h(s) = \frac{3}{4\h}\!\left(1 - \frac{s^2}{\h^2}\right)
             \mathbf{1}_{\{|s|\le\h\}}.
  \label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
It is a symmetric, non-negative, compactly supported kernel that integrates to
one and minimises asymptotic mean integrated squared error among all kernels.

\paragraph{Kernel CDF.}
The antiderivative of $K_\h$, needed for interval convolution (Section~\ref{sec:intervals}), is
\begin{equation}
  F_K(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} K_\h(s)\,ds =
  \begin{cases}
    0 & x < -\h, \\[4pt]
    \dfrac{3}{4}\!\left(\dfrac{x}{\h} - \dfrac{x^3}{3\h^3} + \dfrac{2}{3}\right)
      & |x| \le \h, \\[4pt]
    1 & x > \h.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:Fk}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Helper \texttt{epanechnikovIntegral}$(u_{\ell}, u_r)$.}
Returns the partial mass of the kernel over a normalised sub-interval:
\begin{equation}
  \texttt{epanechnikovIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r)
    = \int_{u_\ell}^{u_r} \tfrac{3}{4}(1 - u^2)\,du
    = \tfrac{3}{4}\!\left[u - \tfrac{u^3}{3}\right]_{u_\ell}^{u_r}.
  \label{eq:epInt}
\end{equation}
On full support $[-1,1]$ this equals $1$.  It is used for boundary correction
(Section~\ref{sec:boundary}).

\paragraph{Helper \texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}$(u_{\ell}, u_r)$.}
Returns the squared-kernel integral needed for $\teff$:
\begin{equation}
  \texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r)
    = \int_{u_\ell}^{u_r}\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^2(1-u^2)^2 du
    = \tfrac{9}{16}\!\left[u - \tfrac{2u^3}{3} + \tfrac{u^5}{5}\right]_{u_\ell}^{u_r}.
  \label{eq:ep2Int}
\end{equation}
On full support $[-1,1]$ this equals $3/5$.

\section{KDE Estimate}

Let the data consist of $N$ point events at times $t_i$ with counts $n_i$,
and independently of $L$ uniformly distributed sample intervals
$[a_\ell, b_\ell]$ with counts $c_\ell$ (Section~\ref{sec:intervals}).
Let $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{end}}$ be the overall data
extents, covering both point events and interval endpoints.  A recorded
zero-count interval extends this observation range without adding count
density.  This preserves leading and trailing zero-count exposure and
allows a positive upper confidence limit for an entirely zero-count
recording.  Point-only input still needs explicit observation information
to distinguish recording time from the first and last detected event.

The boundary-corrected KDE estimate at query time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  \hat\lambda(t)
    = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} n_i\, K_\h(t-t_i)
            \;+\; \Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t)}
           {\km(t)},
  \label{eq:kde}
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t)$ is the interval contribution (Section
\ref{sec:intervals}) and $\km(t)$ is the boundary-correction mass (Section
\ref{sec:boundary}).  The estimate is in cps; multiplication by the
calibration factor $\alpha$ $[\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}/\mathrm{cps}]$ gives dose rate.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Point Events: Sliding-Window Accumulator}
\label{sec:point}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{estimateDoseRateHelper}.}

\medskip\noindent
For a query $t$, only events with $|t - t_i| \le \h$ contribute.  Introducing
the numerical-stability offset $\tau = t - o$ and
$\tau_i = t_i - o$, where $o$ is a nearby query time, the point-event numerator of
\eqref{eq:kde} is
\begin{align}
  \sum_{|t-t_i|\le\h} n_i K_\h(t-t_i)
  &= \frac{3}{4\h}\sum_{|t-t_i|\le\h} n_i\!\left(1 - \frac{(\tau-\tau_i)^2}{\h^2}\right)
  \nonumber\\
  &= \frac{3}{4\h}\!\left(S_0 - \frac{\tau^2 S_0 - 2\tau S_1 + S_2}{\h^2}\right),
  \label{eq:pointsum}
\end{align}
where $S_k = \sum_{|t-t_i|\le\h} n_i\,\tau_i^k$ for $k=0,1,2$.

As $t$ moves rightward over the sorted query array, the window $[t-\h, t+\h]$
slides, and $S_0, S_1, S_2$ are updated in $O(1)$ per admitted or evicted
event.  Binary searches skip data outside the query windows; the moment
origin is reset on a seek and periodically during a long sweep.  This
changes neither the kernel nor the observation bounds.  The arithmetic
cost is linear in the query count and the entries actually visited;
indexing costs are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:complexity}.

\section{Interval Events: Step Decomposition}
\label{sec:intervals}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{addSampleInterval} and
\texttt{estimateDoseRateHelper}.}

\medskip\noindent
An interval $[a, b]$ with $c$ counts models a uniform count-rate density
$w = c/(b-a)$ $[\mathrm{cps}]$.  Its exact contribution to the KDE numerator
is
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{[a,b]}(t) = w\int_a^b K_\h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
                     = w\bigl[F_K(t-a) - F_K(t-b)\bigr].
  \label{eq:ivContrib}
\end{equation}
This expresses the interval as two \emph{step events}: a rising step at $a$
with weight $+w$ and a falling step at $b$ with weight $-w$:
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{[a,b]}(t) = (+w)\,F_K(t - a) + (-w)\,F_K(t - b).
  \label{eq:stepDecomp}
\end{equation}
For $M$ step events at times $\{t_j\}$ with weights $\{w_j\}$ (so $2L = M$),
the total interval contribution is
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_j\, F_K(t - t_j).
  \label{eq:ivTotal}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Three-Phase Lifecycle}

As $t$ sweeps rightward, step event $j$ passes through three phases:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Phase & Condition & Contribution \\
\hline
Inactive    & $t < t_j - \h$ & $0$ \\
Transitioning & $|t - t_j| \le \h$ & $w_j F_K(t-t_j)$ \quad (cubic in $t$) \\
Completed   & $t > t_j + \h$ & $w_j \cdot 1$ \quad (constant) \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The \emph{completed} events contribute only their weights summed into a scalar
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$, maintained by adding $w_j$ when event $j$ exits the
transitioning window.

\subsection{Transitioning Accumulator Expansion}
\label{sec:tkAccum}

For transitioning events, introduce $\tau_j = t_j - o$ and
$\tau = t - o$, using the same local origin as the point accumulators.
Substituting \eqref{eq:Fk} and expanding
$(t - t_j)^3 = (\tau - \tau_j)^3$:
\begin{equation}
  w_j\,F_K(t-t_j)
  = \frac{3}{4}\, w_j \!\left(\frac{\tau - \tau_j}{\h}
      - \frac{(\tau-\tau_j)^3}{3\h^3} + \frac{2}{3}\right).
\end{equation}
Collecting by powers of $\tau_j$ and defining moment accumulators
\begin{equation}
  \tilde T_k = \sum_{\text{transitioning}\,j} w_j\,\tau_j^k,
  \quad k = 0,1,2,3,
  \label{eq:Tk}
\end{equation}
the sum over all transitioning events evaluates to
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{trans}}(\tau)
  = \frac{3}{4}\!\left[
      \tilde T_0\!\left(\frac{\tau}{\h} - \frac{\tau^3}{3\h^3} + \frac{2}{3}\right)
    + \tilde T_1\!\left(\frac{\tau^2}{\h^3} - \frac{1}{\h}\right)
    - \tilde T_2\,\frac{\tau}{\h^3}
    + \tilde T_3\,\frac{1}{3\h^3}
  \right].
  \label{eq:transTerm}
\end{equation}
The accumulators $\tilde T_0,\ldots,\tilde T_3$ are updated in $O(1)$ per
admitted or evicted step event, exactly mirroring the $S_0,S_1,S_2$ treatment
of point events.  The total interval contribution is then
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t) = \Sigma_{\mathrm{done}} + \Lambda_{\mathrm{trans}}(\tau).
\end{equation}

The sliding window for step events admits event $j$ when
$t_j - \h \le t$ and evicts it (to $\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$) when
$t_j + \h \le t$.  Admission is processed before eviction so that a step
event whose entire kernel support has already passed is immediately routed to
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$ without corrupting the $\tilde T_k$ accumulators.

Coincident step events can merge, so $M\le 2L$ for $L$ intervals.  The
arithmetic work of a full sweep remains $O(N+M+Q)$; the range-selection
and cache costs are described in Sections~\ref{sec:queryRange}
and~\ref{sec:complexity}.

\section{Boundary Correction}
\label{sec:boundary}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{estimateDoseRateHelper} and
\texttt{getConfidenceIntervals}.}

\medskip\noindent
Near the endpoints of the data, the kernel window $[t-\h, t+\h]$ extends
beyond the data range $[t_{\mathrm{start}}, t_{\mathrm{end}}]$.  The
\emph{renormalisation} (boundary) correction divides the KDE numerator by
the fraction of the kernel that lies within the data range.

Using the substitution $u = (t - \tau)/\h$ (so $\tau = t_{\mathrm{start}}$
maps to $u_r = (t - t_{\mathrm{start}})/\h$ and $\tau = t_{\mathrm{end}}$
maps to $u_\ell = (t - t_{\mathrm{end}})/\h$), the boundary-correction kernel
mass is
\begin{equation}
  \km(t) = \int_{t_{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{\mathrm{end}}} K_\h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
          = \texttt{epanechnikovIntegral}(u_\ell,\, u_r),
  \label{eq:km}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  u_\ell = \max\!\left(-1,\;\frac{t - t_{\mathrm{end}}}{\h}\right),\qquad
  u_r    = \min\!\left(+1,\;\frac{t - t_{\mathrm{start}}}{\h}\right).
  \label{eq:ulu}
\end{equation}
For $t$ in the interior (both kernel edges inside the data range),
$u_\ell = -1$, $u_r = +1$, and $\km = 1$.

It is essential that $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{end}}$ span
\emph{interval endpoints} as well as point-event timestamps; otherwise the
correction overestimates the boundary effect at the edges of long intervals.

\section{Confidence Intervals}
\label{sec:ci}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{getConfidenceIntervals}.}

\subsection{Effective Observation Time}

Locally, the KDE behaves as if $\neff$ counts were observed over an
equivalent observation window of duration $\teff$.  By matching the
asymptotic Poisson variance of the KDE to that of a direct observation,
\begin{equation}
  \teff = \frac{\km(t)^2}{\displaystyle\int_{t_{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{\mathrm{end}}}
             K_\h^2(t-\tau)\,d\tau}.
  \label{eq:teff}
\end{equation}
The denominator is computed by substituting $u=(t-\tau)/\h$, giving
$d\tau = -\h\,du$:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{t_{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{\mathrm{end}}} K_\h^2(t-\tau)\,d\tau
  = \frac{1}{\h}\;\texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r),
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
  \teff = \frac{\h\;\km(t)^2}{\texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r)}.
  \label{eq:teffCode}
\end{equation}
For an interior point with full kernel support, this reduces to
$\teff = \h \cdot 1^2 / (3/5) = 5\h/3$.

\subsection{Effective Count and Garwood Interval}

The effective count is
\begin{equation}
  \neff = \hat\lambda(t)\;\teff,
  \label{eq:neff}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\lambda(t)$ is the cps estimate from \eqref{eq:kde}.

The \emph{Garwood} exact Poisson confidence interval at confidence level
$1-\alpha$ for an observed integer count $n$ is
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\chi^2_{2n,\,\alpha/2}}{2},\quad
        \frac{\chi^2_{2(n+1),\,1-\alpha/2}}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
expressed here as bounds on the true count.  Dividing by $\teff$ and
multiplying by the calibration factor $\alpha$ converts to $\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}$.

Because $\neff$ is in general non-integer, the bounds are linearly
interpolated between the two bracketing integers
$n_F = \lfloor\neff\rfloor$ and $n_C = n_F + 1$ using weight
$\xi = \neff - n_F$:
\begin{align}
  \chi_L &= \chi^2_{2n_F,\,0.025}
           + \xi\!\left(\chi^2_{2n_C,\,0.025}
                        - \chi^2_{2n_F,\,0.025}\right), \\[4pt]
  \chi_R &= \chi^2_{2(n_F+1),\,0.975}
           + \xi\!\left(\chi^2_{2(n_C+1),\,0.975}
                        - \chi^2_{2(n_F+1),\,0.975}\right).
  \label{eq:chiInterp}
\end{align}
The dose-rate confidence bounds are
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^{\pm}\usvh
  = \frac{\chi_{L,R}}{2\,\teff}\cdot\alpha.
  \label{eq:ciBounds}
\end{equation}

\section{Adaptive Bandwidth with Reflection}
\label{sec:adaptive}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{estimateAdaptiveDoseRate} and
\texttt{getAdaptiveConfidenceIntervals}.}

\subsection{Reflected Kernel}

Let $A$ and $B$ be the observation bounds, including any supplied
\texttt{tEndOverride}, and let $D=B-A>0$.  The adaptive estimator uses
reflection in both its pilot and final pass.  This is a separate boundary
policy from the fixed estimator's kernel-mass division.

Use coordinates $u=t-A$ and $v=s-A$ in $[0,D]$.  Define the periodised
Epanechnikov kernel and its reflected counterpart by
\begin{align}
  P_h(z)&=\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z}K_h(z+2kD),\\
  Q_h(t,s)&=P_h(u-v)+P_h(u+v).
  \label{eq:reflectedKernel}
\end{align}
The first term contains translated original sources; the second contains
translated mirror images.  Every sum is finite because $K_h$ has compact
support.  When $h\le D$, ordinary reflections at the two endpoints suffice.
When $h>D$, the same formula includes all repeated reflections; merely
adding one image at each endpoint would miss contributions.

For a common prescribed bandwidth,
\begin{equation}
  \int_A^B Q_h(t,s)\,ds=1,
  \qquad
  \int_A^B Q_h(t,s)\,dt=1.
  \label{eq:reflectionMass}
\end{equation}
Thus reflection preserves a constant rate in expectation on a fixed,
fully observed range and preserves the integrated mass of each source.
There is no kernel-mass denominator.  It does not remove ordinary
smoothing bias, and it imposes a horizontal tangent at each endpoint.
Data-dependent or source-dependent bandwidths do not inherit a general
unbiasedness guarantee from \eqref{eq:reflectionMass}.

The adaptive estimator is defined on $[A,B]$.  Queries outside that range
return zero means and bounds; reflected copies do not represent additional
observation time or an indefinitely repeated signal outside the recording.
A nonpositive observation duration also gives zero outputs.

\subsection{Pilot Estimate and Per-Sample Scale}

The TimePlot scale slider supplies $h_0>0$.  First, evaluate a
fixed-bandwidth \emph{reflected} pilot at the original source times:
\begin{equation}
  D_0(x)=\alpha\left[
      \sum_i n_i Q_{h_0}(x,t_i)
      +\sum_\ell w_\ell\int_{a_\ell}^{b_\ell}Q_{h_0}(x,s)\,ds
    \right],
  \qquad w_\ell=\frac{c_\ell}{b_\ell-a_\ell}.
  \label{eq:reflectedPilot}
\end{equation}
Here $\alpha$ is inverse detector sensitivity.  The source bandwidth is
\begin{equation}
  h(x)=\min\!\left(h_0,
       \max\!\left(\frac{h_0}{10},
              h_0\frac{0.1\,\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}}{D_0(x)}\right)\right).
  \label{eq:adaptiveScale}
\end{equation}
A zero pilot dose selects $h_0$.  Point events use $x=t_i$; an interval
uses its original midpoint $x=(a_\ell+b_\ell)/2$.  The midpoint selects
only the bandwidth: interval counts remain uniformly spread over their
original interval and are integrated analytically.  Every reflected copy
inherits its original source's bandwidth.  It is not a new observation
and does not receive an independently selected bandwidth.

The final rate is
\begin{equation}
  \hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}(t)
  =\sum_i n_i Q_{h_i}(t,t_i)
    +\sum_\ell w_\ell\int_{a_\ell}^{b_\ell}Q_{h_\ell}(t,s)\,ds.
  \label{eq:adaptiveMean}
\end{equation}
Multiplication by $\alpha$ converts cps to dose rate.  Original interval
identities are retained separately from merged step events so their
midpoints and selected bandwidths remain available.

\subsection{A Bounded Number of Quadratic Kernel Pieces}
\label{sec:adaptivePolynomials}

Explicitly allocating every repeated image would cost $O(h/D)$ for short
recordings.  Instead, $P_h(z)$ is represented by a few quadratic pieces
on $z\in[-D,2D]$, which covers both arguments in
\eqref{eq:reflectedKernel}.  Its only internal breakpoints are
$z=\pm h-2kD$.  These form two arithmetic progressions of period $2D$,
so the number of breakpoints on this range is bounded independently of
$h/D$.

Within one piece, choose a reference offset $z_0$.  The active image
indices are consecutive integers:
\begin{equation}
  k_- =\left\lceil\frac{-h-z_0}{2D}\right\rceil,
  \qquad
  k_+ =\left\lfloor\frac{h-z_0}{2D}\right\rfloor.
\end{equation}
For $n=k_+-k_-+1>0$, set
\begin{equation}
  \bar k=\frac{k_-+k_+}{2},\quad
  z_c=\frac{z_0+2D\bar k}{h},\quad
  d=\frac{2D}{h},\quad f=\frac{3n}{4h}.
\end{equation}
Using the centred integer moment
$\sum_{k=k_-}^{k_+}(k-\bar k)^2=n(n^2-1)/12$ gives
\begin{equation}
  P_h(z_0+x)
   =f\left[1-z_c^2-\frac{(nd)^2-d^2}{12}
              -\frac{2z_c}{h}x-\frac{x^2}{h^2}\right].
  \label{eq:periodicCoefficients}
\end{equation}
An empty index range contributes zero.  This centred expression avoids
subtracting large uncentred sums of image-index powers.  In particular,
the product $nd$ is formed before squaring.  No loop over all images is
needed, even when the bandwidth greatly exceeds the observation duration.

\subsection{Exact Interval Integration and Scalar Coefficients}

For a point source, $Q_h(t,t_i)$ is piecewise quadratic and its square is
piecewise quartic.  For an interval, split the source-coordinate integral
where either argument of $P_h$ crosses a kernel-piece boundary $c$:
\begin{equation}
  v=u-c,\qquad v=c-u.
\end{equation}
Together with the original interval endpoints, these are affine functions
of query time.  Their ordering changes only when an endpoint is crossed
or two such lines intersect.  Split the query range at those crossings.
On each resulting query piece, all integration limits are affine and each
kernel expression has a fixed quadratic form.

Three-point Gauss--Legendre integration on every source-coordinate cell
is exact here: the mean integrand has degree two and the variance
integrand has degree four.  The nodes and weights are
\begin{equation}
  \xi_j\in\{-\sqrt{3/5},0,\sqrt{3/5}\},
  \qquad \omega_j\in\{5/9,8/9,5/9\}.
\end{equation}
This is an algebraic integration identity, not discretisation of the
recorded counts.

To construct coefficients around a nearby query origin, write $x=t-o$.
A cell's half-width is $H(x)=H_0+H_1x$, and each transformed quadrature
node is affine in $x$.  The \emph{sum} of the two reflected kernel terms at
that node is therefore
\begin{equation}
  p(x)=p_0+p_1x+p_2x^2.
\end{equation}
After multiplying by the cell half-width, quadrature weight, and interval
density, its mean contribution has coefficients proportional to
\begin{equation}
  (H_0p_0,\quad H_0p_1+H_1p_0,\quad
    H_0p_2+H_1p_1,\quad H_1p_2).
\end{equation}
For variance first square $p(x)$:
\begin{equation}
  (a_0,a_1,a_2,a_3,a_4)
  =(p_0^2,2p_0p_1,p_1^2+2p_0p_2,2p_1p_2,p_2^2).
\end{equation}
The variance coefficients are then proportional to
\begin{equation}
 (H_0a_0,\ H_0a_1+H_1a_0,\ H_0a_2+H_1a_1,\
 H_0a_3+H_1a_2,\ H_0a_4+H_1a_3,\ H_1a_4).
\end{equation}
Thus interval means are at most cubic and variances at most quintic.
Named scalar coefficients suffice; no general polynomial-expression
framework is needed.

Each source's piece beginnings and endings are sorted independently.
The sweep adds and removes the corresponding coefficients, and evaluates
the accumulated mean and variance with Horner's rule.  Boundary queries
use the same polynomial mechanism as interior queries.  Local coordinates
and rebasing control floating-point cancellation; numerical safeguards do
not alter the mathematical estimator.

\subsection{Variance and Confidence Intervals}

Mirrors are perfectly linked to their original source.  They must be
summed \emph{before} squaring.  With selected bandwidths treated as fixed,
the local Poisson-event variance estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat V(t)=\sum_i n_i Q_{h_i}(t,t_i)^2
      +\sum_\ell w_\ell\int_{a_\ell}^{b_\ell}Q_{h_\ell}(t,s)^2\,ds.
  \label{eq:adaptiveVariance}
\end{equation}
For one original and one image this includes
$2K_{\mathrm{original}}K_{\mathrm{image}}$.  Treating their counts as
independent would omit that cross term.  At an isolated endpoint the two
kernels coincide, so omitting it underestimates variance by a factor of
two.  Squaring the complete node value in the coefficient construction
above includes every image cross term, also for repeated reflections.

The maximum-bandwidth reference exposure is
\begin{equation}
  T_0(t)=\left[\int_A^B Q_{h_0}(t,s)^2\,ds\right]^{-1}.
  \label{eq:reflectedExposure}
\end{equation}
The same exact interval integration evaluates this integral.  It gives
$5h_0/3$ in a full-support interior and $5h_0/6$ at an isolated boundary.
For $h_0\gg D$, the reflected kernel approaches $1/D$, and $T_0$
approaches the actual observation duration $D$.

For positive mean and variance, use
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{eff,ad}}(t)
    =\min\!\left(\frac{\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}(t)}{\widehat V(t)},T_0(t)\right),
  \qquad n_{\mathrm{eff,ad}}(t)=\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}(t)T_{\mathrm{eff,ad}}(t).
  \label{eq:adaptiveEffective}
\end{equation}
For zero mean or variance use $T_0$.  The cap regularises sparse tails;
it is not a proof of confidence coverage.  At an in-range query with zero
local counts and positive observation duration, the lower bound is zero
and the upper bound is approximately $3.68888\alpha/T_0$.
Use the same asymmetric, interpolated chi-square construction as
\eqref{eq:chiInterp}.  The chi-square utility uses tabulated low-count
quantiles and the Wilson--Hilferty approximation above its table.

These are approximate effective-count intervals.  Their calculation does
not propagate pilot or bandwidth-selection uncertainty and does not
correct smoothing bias.  Moreover, for an aggregated interval count
$c_\ell$, the implemented local-event variance
$w_\ell\int Q^2$ is not the bin-count sampling variance
$c_\ell[(b_\ell-a_\ell)^{-1}\int Q]^2$.  The former retains uncertainty
from unresolved event positions and is at least as large for prescribed
weights.  Neither this modelling choice nor the fractional-count
interpolation provides the exact coverage guarantee of a Garwood interval
for a directly observed integer Poisson count.

\section{Exact Query-Range Selection}
\label{sec:queryRange}

Let the sorted requested query times lie in $[x_L,x_R]$.  Compact support
allows the convolution to skip most of a long track without changing its
mathematical result.  The original observation extent $[A,B]$ must still
be used in all boundary treatments and confidence exposures: selecting
sources for computation does not shorten the measurement.

\subsection{Fixed Bandwidth}

For bandwidth $h_0$, a point can contribute only if
\begin{equation}
  t_i\in[x_L-h_0,x_R+h_0].
\end{equation}
An interval $[a,b]$ can contribute only if it intersects that expanded
range, equivalently $a\le x_R+h_0$ and $b\ge x_L-h_0$.  An interval is
retained with its original density and endpoints; filtering solely by its
midpoint or requiring both endpoints inside the range would be incorrect.
Thus $\delta=h_0$ is sufficient for fixed KDE, with interval selection
understood as an intersection test.

The implementation binary-searches the first point in the initial kernel
window.  For the merged step representation it also preserves the
completed-step density
\begin{equation}
  P(x_L-h_0)=\sum_{t_j\le x_L-h_0}w_j.
\end{equation}
This initializes $\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$, while only later step transitions
need to enter the polynomial accumulators.  Dropping all earlier step
events without retaining this sum would lose the contribution of a long
interval spanning the window.  Cumulative step weights are cached lazily
and invalidated from the earliest modified entry on insertion, merging,
or pruning.

Disjoint successive query windows cause another binary seek, so data
between these windows need not enter the convolution.  The local moment
origin is also reset after a sweep of more than $32h_0$ to avoid large
powers of elapsed track time.  Admission precedes eviction, and empty
accumulators are reset to zero; a sparse query cannot accidentally retain
already-expired point kernels.

\subsection{Adaptive Candidates and Pilot Dependencies}

Reflection does not invalidate compact-support source selection inside
the observation range.  For $t,s\in[A,B]$, every reflected path has length
at least $|t-s|$.  Therefore a source can contribute only when its
\emph{original} position is within its bandwidth of a requested query.
Since $h_i\le h_0$, the same $h_0$-expanded query hull is sufficient.
Intervals are retained by overlap, with their original endpoints and
density.  A cached interval-overlap index finds long spanning intervals
without scanning every interval.

Only final candidates need pilot bandwidths.  Their representative times
are point timestamps and original interval midpoints.  The reflected
pilot at each such time $c$ requires original point inputs in
$[c-h_0,c+h_0]$ and interval inputs overlapping that window.  Those pilot
inputs do not themselves require another pilot.  All reflection and
exposure calculations continue to use the complete observation bounds;
filtering inputs for a query never shortens the recording.

For point-only data a $2h_0$ expansion of the requested query hull covers
both passes.  For arbitrary-length intervals there is no universal finite
expansion depending only on $h_0$: a contributing interval's original
midpoint can be arbitrarily far from the query, and its pilot depends on
data near that midpoint.  A safe pilot-input hull spans the smallest and
largest candidate representative times, expanded by $h_0$.

\section{Summary of Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

Let $N$ be the number of point events, $L$ the number of original
intervals, $M\le2L$ the number of merged step events, and $Q$ the number
of queries.  For the fixed estimator, let $N_w$ and $M_w$ count entries
actually visited by the local accumulators, $J$ be the number of seeks
including numerical rebases, and $B_P$ be the work to allocate or extend
the cumulative step-weight cache.  The query cost is
\begin{equation}
  O\!\left(Q+N_w+M_w+J\log(N+1)+J\log(M+1)+B_P\right).
\end{equation}
The cache requires at most $O(M)$ initial work and storage; repeated
queries without data changes reuse its valid prefix.  Full-range sweeps
retain the original polynomial accumulation; narrow or disjoint ranges
skip unrelated entries.

For an adaptive reflected pass, let $S$ be the number of selected
sources and $Q$ the number of requested queries.  The periodised kernel
has a bounded number of pieces independent of $h/D$.  Consequently each
source produces a bounded number of mean/variance pieces, including
interval endpoint crossings and reflected cross terms.  Piece construction
is $O(S)$, sorting support events is $O(S\log(S+1))$, and a polynomial sweep
costs $O(S+Q)$ apart from numerical rebasing or exceptional fallback.
There is no routine per-active-source loop at boundary queries.

The complete adaptive calculation also pays for its reflected pilot and
source selection.  With $S_c$ final candidates, pilot cost $C_P$, and
$V_I$ visited interval-index nodes, the ordinary cost is
\begin{equation}
 O\!\left(\log(N+1)+V_I+C_P+S_c\log(S_c+1)+Q\right),
\end{equation}
plus any numerical safeguard work.  Repeated reflections in a short
recording do not introduce a factor $h_0/D$: their contributions are
summed through the centred integer moments in
\eqref{eq:periodicCoefficients}.  Final-pass temporary storage is
$O(S_c+Q)$, in addition to pilot storage and the reusable indexes.

Building the interval index initially costs $O(L\log(L+1))$ time and
$O(L)$ space; it is reused until interval insertion, pruning, or clearing
invalidates it.  Its overlap search has conservative bound
$V_I=O((L_c+1)\log(L+1))$, where $L_c$ is the number of reported intervals.

Snapshots share an already-built immutable interval index and copy the
valid step-weight prefix.  They do not build either cache on the source
estimator: repeated fresh snapshots of an uncached source still pay the
initial cache-building costs.  Copying the underlying data remains
$O(N+M+L)$; this optimization accelerates convolution and source selection,
not ingestion or full-dataset snapshot creation.
