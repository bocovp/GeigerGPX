\noindent
The \texttt{KernelDensityEstimator} Kotlin class converts a stream of
Geiger--Müller counts into a continuous dose-rate curve
$\hat\lambda(t)$ in $\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}$, together with Garwood Poisson
confidence intervals based on an effective count and exposure.  The original
fixed-bandwidth estimator and the optional adaptive estimator use separate
calculation paths.  Garwood intervals are exact for integer Poisson counts;
their effective-count application to a smoothed KDE is an approximation.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Epanechnikov Kernel}

The class uses the \emph{Epanechnikov} kernel with bandwidth $\h > 0$:
\begin{equation}
  K_\h(s) = \frac{3}{4\h}\!\left(1 - \frac{s^2}{\h^2}\right)
             \mathbf{1}_{\{|s|\le\h\}}.
  \label{eq:kernel}
\end{equation}
It is a symmetric, non-negative, compactly supported kernel that integrates to
one and minimises asymptotic mean integrated squared error among all kernels.

\paragraph{Kernel CDF.}
The antiderivative of $K_\h$, needed for interval convolution (Section~\ref{sec:intervals}), is
\begin{equation}
  F_K(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} K_\h(s)\,ds =
  \begin{cases}
    0 & x < -\h, \\[4pt]
    \dfrac{3}{4}\!\left(\dfrac{x}{\h} - \dfrac{x^3}{3\h^3} + \dfrac{2}{3}\right)
      & |x| \le \h, \\[4pt]
    1 & x > \h.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:Fk}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Helper \texttt{epanechnikovIntegral}$(u_{\ell}, u_r)$.}
Returns the partial mass of the kernel over a normalised sub-interval:
\begin{equation}
  \texttt{epanechnikovIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r)
    = \int_{u_\ell}^{u_r} \tfrac{3}{4}(1 - u^2)\,du
    = \tfrac{3}{4}\!\left[u - \tfrac{u^3}{3}\right]_{u_\ell}^{u_r}.
  \label{eq:epInt}
\end{equation}
On full support $[-1,1]$ this equals $1$.  It is used for boundary correction
(Section~\ref{sec:boundary}).

\paragraph{Helper \texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}$(u_{\ell}, u_r)$.}
Returns the squared-kernel integral needed for $\teff$:
\begin{equation}
  \texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r)
    = \int_{u_\ell}^{u_r}\!\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^2(1-u^2)^2 du
    = \tfrac{9}{16}\!\left[u - \tfrac{2u^3}{3} + \tfrac{u^5}{5}\right]_{u_\ell}^{u_r}.
  \label{eq:ep2Int}
\end{equation}
On full support $[-1,1]$ this equals $3/5$.

\section{KDE Estimate}

Let the data consist of $N$ point events at times $t_i$ with counts $n_i$,
and independently of $L$ uniformly distributed sample intervals
$[a_\ell, b_\ell]$ with counts $c_\ell$ (Section~\ref{sec:intervals}).
Let $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{end}}$ be the overall data
extents, covering both point events and interval endpoints.

The boundary-corrected KDE estimate at query time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  \hat\lambda(t)
    = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N} n_i\, K_\h(t-t_i)
            \;+\; \Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t)}
           {\km(t)},
  \label{eq:kde}
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t)$ is the interval contribution (Section
\ref{sec:intervals}) and $\km(t)$ is the boundary-correction mass (Section
\ref{sec:boundary}).  The estimate is in cps; multiplication by the
calibration factor $\alpha$ $[\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}/\mathrm{cps}]$ gives dose rate.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Point Events: Sliding-Window Accumulator}
\label{sec:point}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{estimateDoseRateHelper}.}

\medskip\noindent
For a query $t$, only events with $|t - t_i| \le \h$ contribute.  Introducing
the numerical-stability offset $\tau = t - o$ and
$\tau_i = t_i - o$, where $o$ is a nearby query time, the point-event numerator of
\eqref{eq:kde} is
\begin{align}
  \sum_{|t-t_i|\le\h} n_i K_\h(t-t_i)
  &= \frac{3}{4\h}\sum_{|t-t_i|\le\h} n_i\!\left(1 - \frac{(\tau-\tau_i)^2}{\h^2}\right)
  \nonumber\\
  &= \frac{3}{4\h}\!\left(S_0 - \frac{\tau^2 S_0 - 2\tau S_1 + S_2}{\h^2}\right),
  \label{eq:pointsum}
\end{align}
where $S_k = \sum_{|t-t_i|\le\h} n_i\,\tau_i^k$ for $k=0,1,2$.

As $t$ moves rightward over the sorted query array, the window $[t-\h, t+\h]$
slides, and $S_0, S_1, S_2$ are updated in $O(1)$ per admitted or evicted
event.  Binary searches skip data outside the query windows; the moment
origin is reset on a seek and periodically during a long sweep.  This
changes neither the kernel nor the observation bounds.  The arithmetic
cost is linear in the query count and the entries actually visited;
indexing costs are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:complexity}.

\section{Interval Events: Step Decomposition}
\label{sec:intervals}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{addSampleInterval} and
\texttt{estimateDoseRateHelper}.}

\medskip\noindent
An interval $[a, b]$ with $c$ counts models a uniform count-rate density
$w = c/(b-a)$ $[\mathrm{cps}]$.  Its exact contribution to the KDE numerator
is
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{[a,b]}(t) = w\int_a^b K_\h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
                     = w\bigl[F_K(t-a) - F_K(t-b)\bigr].
  \label{eq:ivContrib}
\end{equation}
This expresses the interval as two \emph{step events}: a rising step at $a$
with weight $+w$ and a falling step at $b$ with weight $-w$:
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{[a,b]}(t) = (+w)\,F_K(t - a) + (-w)\,F_K(t - b).
  \label{eq:stepDecomp}
\end{equation}
For $M$ step events at times $\{t_j\}$ with weights $\{w_j\}$ (so $2L = M$),
the total interval contribution is
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{M} w_j\, F_K(t - t_j).
  \label{eq:ivTotal}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Three-Phase Lifecycle}

As $t$ sweeps rightward, step event $j$ passes through three phases:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\hline
Phase & Condition & Contribution \\
\hline
Inactive    & $t < t_j - \h$ & $0$ \\
Transitioning & $|t - t_j| \le \h$ & $w_j F_K(t-t_j)$ \quad (cubic in $t$) \\
Completed   & $t > t_j + \h$ & $w_j \cdot 1$ \quad (constant) \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
The \emph{completed} events contribute only their weights summed into a scalar
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$, maintained by adding $w_j$ when event $j$ exits the
transitioning window.

\subsection{Transitioning Accumulator Expansion}
\label{sec:tkAccum}

For transitioning events, introduce $\tau_j = t_j - o$ and
$\tau = t - o$, using the same local origin as the point accumulators.
Substituting \eqref{eq:Fk} and expanding
$(t - t_j)^3 = (\tau - \tau_j)^3$:
\begin{equation}
  w_j\,F_K(t-t_j)
  = \frac{3}{4}\, w_j \!\left(\frac{\tau - \tau_j}{\h}
      - \frac{(\tau-\tau_j)^3}{3\h^3} + \frac{2}{3}\right).
\end{equation}
Collecting by powers of $\tau_j$ and defining moment accumulators
\begin{equation}
  \tilde T_k = \sum_{\text{transitioning}\,j} w_j\,\tau_j^k,
  \quad k = 0,1,2,3,
  \label{eq:Tk}
\end{equation}
the sum over all transitioning events evaluates to
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{trans}}(\tau)
  = \frac{3}{4}\!\left[
      \tilde T_0\!\left(\frac{\tau}{\h} - \frac{\tau^3}{3\h^3} + \frac{2}{3}\right)
    + \tilde T_1\!\left(\frac{\tau^2}{\h^3} - \frac{1}{\h}\right)
    - \tilde T_2\,\frac{\tau}{\h^3}
    + \tilde T_3\,\frac{1}{3\h^3}
  \right].
  \label{eq:transTerm}
\end{equation}
The accumulators $\tilde T_0,\ldots,\tilde T_3$ are updated in $O(1)$ per
admitted or evicted step event, exactly mirroring the $S_0,S_1,S_2$ treatment
of point events.  The total interval contribution is then
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda_{\mathrm{iv}}(t) = \Sigma_{\mathrm{done}} + \Lambda_{\mathrm{trans}}(\tau).
\end{equation}

The sliding window for step events admits event $j$ when
$t_j - \h \le t$ and evicts it (to $\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$) when
$t_j + \h \le t$.  Admission is processed before eviction so that a step
event whose entire kernel support has already passed is immediately routed to
$\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$ without corrupting the $\tilde T_k$ accumulators.

Coincident step events can merge, so $M\le 2L$ for $L$ intervals.  The
arithmetic work of a full sweep remains $O(N+M+Q)$; the range-selection
and cache costs are described in Sections~\ref{sec:queryRange}
and~\ref{sec:complexity}.

\section{Boundary Correction}
\label{sec:boundary}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{estimateDoseRateHelper} and
\texttt{getConfidenceIntervals}.}

\medskip\noindent
Near the endpoints of the data, the kernel window $[t-\h, t+\h]$ extends
beyond the data range $[t_{\mathrm{start}}, t_{\mathrm{end}}]$.  The
\emph{renormalisation} (boundary) correction divides the KDE numerator by
the fraction of the kernel that lies within the data range.

Using the substitution $u = (t - \tau)/\h$ (so $\tau = t_{\mathrm{start}}$
maps to $u_r = (t - t_{\mathrm{start}})/\h$ and $\tau = t_{\mathrm{end}}$
maps to $u_\ell = (t - t_{\mathrm{end}})/\h$), the boundary-correction kernel
mass is
\begin{equation}
  \km(t) = \int_{t_{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{\mathrm{end}}} K_\h(t-\tau)\,d\tau
          = \texttt{epanechnikovIntegral}(u_\ell,\, u_r),
  \label{eq:km}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation}
  u_\ell = \max\!\left(-1,\;\frac{t - t_{\mathrm{end}}}{\h}\right),\qquad
  u_r    = \min\!\left(+1,\;\frac{t - t_{\mathrm{start}}}{\h}\right).
  \label{eq:ulu}
\end{equation}
For $t$ in the interior (both kernel edges inside the data range),
$u_\ell = -1$, $u_r = +1$, and $\km = 1$.

It is essential that $t_{\mathrm{start}}$ and $t_{\mathrm{end}}$ span
\emph{interval endpoints} as well as point-event timestamps; otherwise the
correction overestimates the boundary effect at the edges of long intervals.

\section{Confidence Intervals}
\label{sec:ci}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{getConfidenceIntervals}.}

\subsection{Effective Observation Time}

Locally, the KDE behaves as if $\neff$ counts were observed over an
equivalent observation window of duration $\teff$.  By matching the
asymptotic Poisson variance of the KDE to that of a direct observation,
\begin{equation}
  \teff = \frac{\km(t)^2}{\displaystyle\int_{t_{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{\mathrm{end}}}
             K_\h^2(t-\tau)\,d\tau}.
  \label{eq:teff}
\end{equation}
The denominator is computed by substituting $u=(t-\tau)/\h$, giving
$d\tau = -\h\,du$:
\begin{equation}
  \int_{t_{\mathrm{start}}}^{t_{\mathrm{end}}} K_\h^2(t-\tau)\,d\tau
  = \frac{1}{\h}\;\texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r),
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
  \teff = \frac{\h\;\km(t)^2}{\texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}(u_\ell, u_r)}.
  \label{eq:teffCode}
\end{equation}
For an interior point with full kernel support, this reduces to
$\teff = \h \cdot 1^2 / (3/5) = 5\h/3$.

\subsection{Effective Count and Garwood Interval}

The effective count is
\begin{equation}
  \neff = \hat\lambda(t)\;\teff,
  \label{eq:neff}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\lambda(t)$ is the cps estimate from \eqref{eq:kde}.

The \emph{Garwood} exact Poisson confidence interval at confidence level
$1-\alpha$ for an observed integer count $n$ is
\begin{equation}
  \left(\frac{\chi^2_{2n,\,\alpha/2}}{2},\quad
        \frac{\chi^2_{2(n+1),\,1-\alpha/2}}{2}\right),
\end{equation}
expressed here as bounds on the true count.  Dividing by $\teff$ and
multiplying by the calibration factor $\alpha$ converts to $\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}$.

Because $\neff$ is in general non-integer, the bounds are linearly
interpolated between the two bracketing integers
$n_F = \lfloor\neff\rfloor$ and $n_C = n_F + 1$ using weight
$\xi = \neff - n_F$:
\begin{align}
  \chi_L &= \chi^2_{2n_F,\,0.025}
           + \xi\!\left(\chi^2_{2n_C,\,0.025}
                        - \chi^2_{2n_F,\,0.025}\right), \\[4pt]
  \chi_R &= \chi^2_{2(n_F+1),\,0.975}
           + \xi\!\left(\chi^2_{2(n_C+1),\,0.975}
                        - \chi^2_{2(n_F+1),\,0.975}\right).
  \label{eq:chiInterp}
\end{align}
The dose-rate confidence bounds are
\begin{equation}
  \lambda^{\pm}\usvh
  = \frac{\chi_{L,R}}{2\,\teff}\cdot\alpha.
  \label{eq:ciBounds}
\end{equation}

\section{Adaptive Bandwidth}
\label{sec:adaptive}

\noindent\textit{Implemented in \texttt{estimateAdaptiveDoseRate} and
\texttt{getAdaptiveConfidenceIntervals}.}

\subsection{Pilot Estimate and Per-Sample Scale}

The adaptive estimator uses the same Epanechnikov kernel
\eqref{eq:kernel}, but assigns a separate bandwidth to each point event and
each original sample interval.  The TimePlot scale slider supplies the
initial bandwidth $h_0>0$ in seconds.  First, the fixed-bandwidth
estimator computes a pilot dose rate
\begin{equation}
  D_0(x) = \alpha\,\hat\lambda_{h_0}(x),
  \qquad D_{\mathrm{bg}}=0.1\,\mu\mathrm{Sv/h},
\end{equation}
where $\alpha$ is the inverse detector sensitivity and the pilot includes
the existing boundary correction.  The bandwidth assigned to a source at
representative time $x$ is
\begin{equation}
  h(x) = \min\!\left(h_0,\;
             \max\!\left(\frac{h_0}{10},\;
                         h_0\frac{D_{\mathrm{bg}}}{D_0(x)}\right)\right).
  \label{eq:adaptiveScale}
\end{equation}
A zero pilot dose uses $h_0$.  Thus $h_0$ is the bandwidth at background
dose and the maximum permitted bandwidth; dose rates at or above
$1\,\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}$ use the minimum $h_0/10$.

For point event $i$, the representative time is $x_i=t_i$ and its bandwidth
is $h_i=h(t_i)$.  For interval $\ell$, the pilot is evaluated only at its
midpoint:
\begin{equation}
  x_\ell=\frac{a_\ell+b_\ell}{2},\qquad h_\ell=h(x_\ell).
\end{equation}
This one bandwidth applies to the whole interval; its counts continue to be
convolved analytically over $[a_\ell,b_\ell]$, rather than concentrated at
the midpoint.  Original intervals are retained separately from the merged
step events, since merging endpoints can discard the interval identities
needed to select these bandwidths.

The representative times of all candidate sources are sorted for the pilot evaluation, and the
resulting bandwidths are held fixed during the second pass.  This is a
sample-point adaptive KDE: the bandwidth belongs to the data source and
does not change as the query time moves.  Candidate and pilot selection
are described in Section~\ref{sec:queryRange}.

\subsection{Estimate and Boundary Correction}

Write $A=t_{\mathrm{start}}$, $B=t_{\mathrm{end}}$, with $B$ extended to
\texttt{tEndOverride} when that is later than the last recorded event.
As in the fixed-bandwidth implementation, boundary correction uses the
clamped query $\bar t=\min(B,\max(A,t))$.  For any bandwidth $h$, define
\begin{equation}
  m_h(t)=\int_A^B K_h(\bar t-s)\,ds
    =\texttt{epanechnikovIntegral}\!\left(
         \max\!\left(-1,\frac{\bar t-B}{h}\right),
         \min\!\left(1,\frac{\bar t-A}{h}\right)\right).
  \label{eq:adaptiveMass}
\end{equation}
Each source is divided by the boundary mass for its own bandwidth.  The
adaptive count-rate estimate is
\begin{equation}
  \hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}(t)
    =\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{n_i K_{h_i}(t-t_i)}{m_{h_i}(t)}
     +\sum_{\ell=1}^L\frac{w_\ell}{m_{h_\ell}(t)}
          \bigl[F_{h_\ell}(t-a_\ell)-F_{h_\ell}(t-b_\ell)\bigr],
  \qquad w_\ell=\frac{c_\ell}{b_\ell-a_\ell}.
  \label{eq:adaptiveMean}
\end{equation}
Here $F_h$ denotes \eqref{eq:Fk} with bandwidth $h$.  In particular, the
$1/h_i$ kernel normalisation is retained for every point.  Multiplication
by $\alpha$ gives dose rate.  A negligible boundary mass contributes zero,
as in the fixed-bandwidth path.

The numerator uses the actual query $t$ even outside the recorded extent;
only the correction mass uses $\bar t$.  This prevents division by a
vanishing boundary mass outside the observation range.  If all bandwidths
coincide, \eqref{eq:adaptiveMean} reduces to the existing fixed-bandwidth
mean.  For varying bandwidths this is a per-source boundary correction;
there is no single common kernel mass to divide out.  It is not a claim of
unbiasedness for arbitrary nonconstant intensity or bandwidth fields.

\subsection{Piecewise Polynomial Accumulation}
\label{sec:adaptivePolynomials}

The second pass evaluates the same mean and variance as
\eqref{eq:adaptiveMean} and \eqref{eq:adaptiveVariance}, using polynomial
accumulators in place of a loop over all contributing sources at every
query.  The pilot, bandwidth limits, boundary correction, and confidence
construction are unchanged.  In this subsection $P$ and $V$ denote raw
mean and variance contributions before boundary normalisation.

\paragraph{Point events.}
A point with count $n$, timestamp $c$, and bandwidth $h$ contributes one
polynomial piece on $[c-h,c+h]$.  With $u=(t-c)/h$,
\begin{equation}
  P(t)=\frac{3n}{4h}(1-u^2),\qquad
  V(t)=\frac{9n}{16h^2}(1-2u^2+u^4).
\end{equation}
The variance weight is $n$, not $n^2$; variances of independent counts
add without cross terms.

\paragraph{Interval events.}
An interval $[a,b]$ of density $w$ and bandwidth $h$ is decomposed into
three positive pieces, separated at
\begin{equation}
  r=\min(a+h,b-h),\qquad s=\max(a+h,b-h).
\end{equation}
Its rising piece lies on $[a-h,r]$, its middle piece on $[r,s]$, and its
falling piece on $[s,b+h]$.  Zero-length pieces can be omitted.  Define
\begin{equation}
  E(z)=\frac{z^2(3-z)}{4},\qquad
  H(z)=\frac{3z^3(20-15z+3z^2)}{80}.
\end{equation}
On the rising piece use $z=(t-a+h)/h$; on the falling piece use
$z=(b+h-t)/h$.  In both cases,
\begin{equation}
  P(t)=wE(z),\qquad V(t)=\frac{w}{h}H(z).
\end{equation}
These are the integrated kernel and integrated squared kernel measured
from a support edge.  The factorisations retain the small quadratic and
cubic masses near that edge without subtracting nearly equal CDF values.

For a long interval, $b-a\ge2h$, the middle is the constant plateau
\begin{equation}
  P(t)=w,\qquad V(t)=\frac{3w}{5h}.
\end{equation}
For a short interval let $d=(b-a)/h<2$ and
$q=(t-(b-h))/h\in[0,2-d]$.  Both kernel limits lie inside the support,
and the middle contribution is
\begin{align}
  P(t)&=w\left[\frac{d^2(3-d)}4
                +\frac{3d(2-d)}4q-\frac{3d}4q^2\right],\\
  V(t)&=\frac{w}{h}\sum_{k=0}^4 v_k q^k,
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  v_0&=\frac{3d^3(20-15d+3d^2)}{80}, &
  v_1&=\frac{9d^2(2-d)^2}{16},\\
  v_2&=\frac{9d(1-d)(2-d)}8, &
  v_3&=-\frac{9d(2-d)}8, &
  v_4&=\frac{9d}{16}.
\end{align}
These factored coefficients are exactly $E(q+d)-E(q)$ and
$H(q+d)-H(q)$, respectively.  The mirrored coordinate
$(a+h-t)/h$ describes the same middle piece.  Consequently degree three
for mean and degree five for variance suffice for every piece, without
interval discretisation.

\paragraph{Sorted piece events.}
Varying bandwidths reorder both support beginnings and endings relative
to the original timestamps.  Piece starts and ends are precalculated and
sorted independently.  At each ascending query, the sweep removes ended
pieces, then admits newly started pieces whose ends have not already
passed.  Skipping such expired pieces handles a query jump across a
complete support without inserting it into the accumulators.

In floating-point arithmetic, events are scheduled at the first requested
query satisfying the original clipping predicate.  The effective outer
support intersects the original admission/eviction interval $[a-h,b+h)$
with $(t-a)(1/h)\ge-1$ and $(t-b)(1/h)<1$; for a point use $a=b=c$.
This excludes a rounded support beginning at which a point's quadratic
would still be negative, and excludes interval tails with no kernel
overlap.  Internal transitions use $(t-a)(1/h)\ge1$ for $a+h$ and
$(t-b)(1/h)\ge-1$ for $b-h$, then are clipped to that effective support.
Each monotone predicate is binary-searched over the sorted query array.
Delaying an event until its first applicable query changes no sampled
value: every earlier query still uses the preceding regime.  This also
handles repeated query timestamps and jumps over entire pieces, while
preserving the original zero contribution after outer-support eviction.

Each admitted or removed piece adds or subtracts a fixed-size coefficient
vector.  Coefficients use a common nearby origin $o$, so a polynomial is
$\sum_{k=0}^d a_k(t-o)^k$.  Translating to $o'=o+\Delta$ gives
\begin{equation}
  a'_k=\sum_{j=k}^d\binom{j}{k}a_j\Delta^{j-k}.
\end{equation}
The initial origin is the first query time.  Periodic rebasing after
$h_{\min}=h_0/10$ rebuilds the coefficients from active nonconstant
pieces, keeping powers of elapsed time bounded.  Queries evaluating a
nonconstant piece span at most $2h_0$ (up to timestamp rounding), so it
participates in a bounded number of rebuilds, at most 21 in exact
arithmetic.  Scheduling an internal event at the next requested query
does not introduce extra evaluations in the intervening gap.  Long
constant plateaus are stored separately and incur no
repeated polynomial work as their duration grows.

\subsection{Exact Boundary Grouping}
\label{sec:adaptiveGroups}

The polynomial optimisation must retain each source's own boundary mass
from \eqref{eq:adaptiveMass}.  Inside a boundary strip that mass depends
on $t$: it is piecewise cubic, or quadratic when both observation edges
truncate the kernel.  A normalised contribution is therefore rational,
not polynomial.  Distinct bandwidths generally give distinct
denominators, so a single fixed-degree polynomial cannot represent their
normalised sum exactly.

Sources are grouped only when their computed bandwidths are exactly
equal; there is no rounding or quantisation of bandwidths.  Let $P_h(t)$
and $V_h(t)$ be a group's polynomial sums, and let $G(t)$ contain the
groups active at $t$.  Boundary-strip queries use
\begin{equation}
  \hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}(t)=\sum_{h\in G(t)}\frac{P_h(t)}{m_h(t)},
  \qquad
  \widehat V(t)=\sum_{h\in G(t)}\frac{V_h(t)}{m_h(t)^2}.
  \label{eq:adaptiveGrouped}
\end{equation}
The group variance is the sum of source variance contributions, never
the square of the group mean.  Groups with negligible boundary mass are
omitted just as in the direct evaluation.

For $A+h_0\le t\le B-h_0$, all masses are one, and a global raw polynomial
sum supplies both results in constant time.  If the observation extent is
shorter than $2h_0$, this interior region is empty.  Outside $[A,B]$, the
clamped query makes each mass constant.  Kernel symmetry gives
\begin{equation}
  m_h(A)=m_h(B)=m_h^{\partial}.
\end{equation}
A second global accumulator, weighted by $1/m_h^{\partial}$ for mean and
$1/(m_h^{\partial})^2$ for variance, therefore serves both exterior
regions in constant time.  Only the interior boundary strips require
per-group evaluation.  This is an algebraic regrouping of the original
per-source correction, not a common-bandwidth approximation.

\subsection{Numerical Stability and Direct Fallback}

Coefficient additions use compensated summation and retain an estimate
of accumulated rounding error, including the history of additions and
removals.  A nominal source-amplitude term also accounts for cancellation
while constructing the constant coefficient, even when evaluation at the
current origin involves no further arithmetic.  Separating constant plateaus permits
nonconstant coefficients to reset to zero when no such pieces remain;
otherwise a tiny residual high-order coefficient could grow across a
long empty gap or plateau.  Periodic rebasing further limits drift.

Boundary groups share one error diagnostic, rather than recomputing it
beside every polynomial value.  For each coefficient, the global
absolute update history dominates both the sum of group histories and
the sum of absolute current group coefficients.  Twice the global
diagnostic therefore covers their separate evaluation estimates, even
when group coefficients cancel in the global sum.  Divide this estimate
by the smallest accepted boundary mass for the mean, or its square for
variance, and include an allowance for the final summation across groups.
These are conservative numerical diagnostics, not certified
floating-point error bounds; the polynomial values and boundary masses
are unchanged by sharing the diagnostic.

Large absolute timestamps need care at piece boundaries as well as in
polynomial powers.  Rounding $a\pm h$ or $b\pm h$ can select a regime one
query too early, particularly for intervals only a few timestamp ulps long.
The query-array searches above preserve the clipping predicates used by
the direct integrals.  Searching adjacent representable timestamps
instead would have no useful iteration bound: subtraction can round a
very large run of neighbouring query values to the same difference near
zero.  Tail coefficients use differences from the original interval
endpoints, rather than treating rounded support boundaries as exact roots.

Even a positive polynomial result may have poor relative accuracy when
an extremely small tail is obtained by cancellation.  When the result is
near the tracked error floor, the implementation reevaluates the active
original sources using the stable point and interval formulas.  This is
a numerical fallback, not the normal per-query path.  It preserves tiny
positive variance contributions needed by the confidence construction.
The piecewise calculation and fallback implement the same exact
convolutions; different floating-point operation order can change the
last bits, so agreement is assessed with numerical tolerances rather
than bit-for-bit identity.

\subsection{Variance and Effective-Count Confidence Intervals}

For the second pass, regard the pilot-selected bandwidths as fixed.  Under
the same independent Poisson count model as the original estimator, a
plug-in estimate of the adaptive rate variance is
\begin{equation}
  \widehat V(t)
    =\sum_{i=1}^N
        \frac{n_i K_{h_i}(t-t_i)^2}{m_{h_i}(t)^2}
     +\sum_{\ell=1}^L
        \frac{w_\ell}{m_{h_\ell}(t)^2}
             \int_{a_\ell}^{b_\ell}K_{h_\ell}(t-s)^2\,ds.
  \label{eq:adaptiveVariance}
\end{equation}
The units are $\mathrm{cps}^2$.  A point holding $n_i$ counts represents
$n_i$ independent counts, so its variance weight is $n_i$, not $n_i^2$.
For an interval, the uniform count density is integrated against the
\emph{squared kernel}.  Squaring the interval's integrated mean would
instead treat the whole interval as one weighted event and would not give
the Poisson variance of the uniform count-density model used here.

The interval term is the same exact squared-kernel integral used in the
fixed-bandwidth derivation:
\begin{align}
  v_\ell^-&=\max\!\left(-1,\frac{t-b_\ell}{h_\ell}\right), &
  v_\ell^+&=\min\!\left(1,\frac{t-a_\ell}{h_\ell}\right),\\
  \int_{a_\ell}^{b_\ell}K_{h_\ell}(t-s)^2\,ds
    &=\frac{1}{h_\ell}\,
       \texttt{epanechnikovSquaredIntegral}(v_\ell^-,v_\ell^+).
\end{align}
The integral is zero when $v_\ell^-\ge v_\ell^+$.  Neither the variance
nor the mean is approximated by interval discretisation.

For a short overlap close to $-1$ or $+1$, subtracting two nearly equal
antiderivative values can lose the small squared-kernel mass to rounding.

The adaptive-only integral helpers therefore evaluate equivalent exact
polynomials around the overlap midpoint.  With
$d=v_\ell^+-v_\ell^-$, $z=(v_\ell^-+v_\ell^+)/2$, and
$p=(1-z)(1+z)=1-z^2$, these are
\begin{align}
  \int_{v_\ell^-}^{v_\ell^+}K(u)\,du
    &=\frac{3d}{4}\left(p-\frac{d^2}{12}\right),\\
  \int_{v_\ell^-}^{v_\ell^+}K(u)^2\,du
    &=\frac{9d}{16}\left(p^2+\frac{(3z^2-1)d^2}{6}
                              +\frac{d^4}{80}\right).
\end{align}
The latter is multiplied by $1/h_\ell$ for the dimensional squared-kernel
integral.  This avoids cancellation without changing the original
fixed-bandwidth integral helpers.

For positive mean and variance, variance matching first gives the
empirical equivalent exposure
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde T(t)=\frac{\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}(t)}{\widehat V(t)}.
\end{equation}
In a sparse tail, a vanishing kernel weight can make this ratio diverge,
even though the data do not justify an arbitrarily long observation time.
The implementation therefore caps the exposure using the background
bandwidth:
\begin{equation}
  T_0(t)=\frac{m_{h_0}(t)^2}
                   {\displaystyle\int_A^B K_{h_0}(\bar t-s)^2\,ds}.
\end{equation}
This is the existing fixed-bandwidth exposure \eqref{eq:teffCode} at
$h_0$ and the clamped query.  The effective exposure and count used for
confidence intervals are
\begin{equation}
  T_{\mathrm{eff,ad}}(t)
     =\min\!\left(\widetilde T(t),T_0(t)\right),
  \qquad
  n_{\mathrm{eff,ad}}(t)
     =\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}(t)\,T_{\mathrm{eff,ad}}(t).
  \label{eq:adaptiveEffective}
\end{equation}
Equivalently, the variance used for matching is increased to
$\max(\widehat V,\hat\lambda_{\mathrm{ad}}/T_0)$.  This is a conservative
exposure regularisation based on the maximum smoothing scale, rather than
a claim that $T_0$ bounds every possible adaptive weighting model.
Use these quantities in the same interpolated Garwood construction
\eqref{eq:chiInterp}--\eqref{eq:ciBounds}.  The calibration factor is applied
only after constructing the rate bounds; equivalently, dose variance is
$\alpha^2$ times the variance used for matching.

At a query with no nonzero contribution, use $T_{\mathrm{eff,ad}}=T_0$
and zero effective count.  The cap makes the confidence bounds approach
these zero-count bounds continuously as the final overlapping kernel
vanishes.  A positive zero-count upper bound is retained wherever the
observation extent gives positive exposure.  Empty datasets or a degenerate
observation extent have no exposure and return zero estimates and bounds.

For a constant Poisson intensity represented by uniform intervals covering
the observation extent, and a common bandwidth $h$, the variance becomes
\begin{equation}
  \widehat V(t)=\frac{\hat\lambda(t)}{m_h(t)^2}
                      \int_A^B K_h(t-s)^2\,ds,
  \qquad t\in[A,B].
\end{equation}
Thus \eqref{eq:adaptiveEffective} reproduces the original effective
exposure, including $5h/3$ for full support.  For discrete point data,
\eqref{eq:adaptiveVariance} is an empirical squared-weight estimate,
whereas the original implementation substitutes a locally homogeneous
intensity into the kernel integral.  Their confidence widths need not
coincide on a finite dataset even when their mean estimates do.

These adaptive intervals are plug-in, effective-count intervals.  They
account for the different bandwidths and boundary weights, but do not
propagate uncertainty from estimating the pilot or selecting bandwidths,
and do not correct smoothing bias.  Therefore they do not have the exact
coverage guarantee of a Garwood interval for an unsmoothed integer Poisson
count.

\section{Exact Query-Range Selection}
\label{sec:queryRange}

Let the sorted requested query times lie in $[x_L,x_R]$.  Compact support
allows the convolution to skip most of a long track without changing its
mathematical result.  The original observation extent $[A,B]$ must still
be used in all boundary masses and confidence exposures: selecting
sources for computation does not shorten the measurement.

\subsection{Fixed Bandwidth}

For bandwidth $h_0$, a point can contribute only if
\begin{equation}
  t_i\in[x_L-h_0,x_R+h_0].
\end{equation}
An interval $[a,b]$ can contribute only if it intersects that expanded
range, equivalently $a\le x_R+h_0$ and $b\ge x_L-h_0$.  An interval is
retained with its original density and endpoints; filtering solely by its
midpoint or requiring both endpoints inside the range would be incorrect.
Thus $\delta=h_0$ is sufficient for fixed KDE, with interval selection
understood as an intersection test.

The implementation binary-searches the first point in the initial kernel
window.  For the merged step representation it also preserves the
completed-step density
\begin{equation}
  P(x_L-h_0)=\sum_{t_j\le x_L-h_0}w_j.
\end{equation}
This initializes $\Sigma_{\mathrm{done}}$, while only later step transitions
need to enter the polynomial accumulators.  Dropping all earlier step
events without retaining this sum would lose the contribution of a long
interval spanning the window.  Cumulative step weights are cached lazily
and invalidated from the earliest modified entry on insertion, merging,
or pruning.

Disjoint successive query windows cause another binary seek, so data
between these windows need not enter the convolution.  The local moment
origin is also reset after a sweep of more than $32h_0$ to avoid large
powers of elapsed track time.  Admission precedes eviction, and empty
accumulators are reset to zero; a sparse query cannot accidentally retain
already-expired point kernels.

\subsection{Adaptive Candidates and Pilot Dependencies}

Since every adaptive bandwidth satisfies $h_i\le h_0$, the same expanded
range identifies all possible final contributors.  Point candidates are
selected by binary search.  Interval candidates are selected by a cached
interval-overlap index: intervals are sorted by start, and a segment tree
stores the largest end in each subtree.  This permits skipping entire
subtrees while retaining long spanning intervals.

Only these candidates need pilot estimates.  Let $C$ be their point
timestamps and original interval midpoints.  The pilot requires point
inputs in
\begin{equation}
  U=\bigcup_{c\in C}[c-h_0,c+h_0],
\end{equation}
and interval inputs intersecting $U$.  The optimized fixed estimator
evaluates these sorted centers directly, seeking across gaps in $U$ and
preserving the completed-step density and original observation extent.
There is no recursive pilot calculation for the pilot inputs themselves.
After selecting bandwidths, candidates whose actual support misses the
query range are also removed before support-boundary sorting.

For point-only data, every candidate center is within
$[x_L-h_0,x_R+h_0]$, so
\begin{equation}
  U\subseteq[x_L-2h_0,x_R+2h_0].
\end{equation}
Thus $\delta=2h_0$ is sufficient for both adaptive passes together and
is needed in the worst case.  A point nearly $h_0$ beyond a query edge
can have its bandwidth changed by another point almost a further $h_0$
away, changing whether its final kernel reaches that edge.

For arbitrary-length intervals, there is no universal finite $\delta$
depending only on $h_0$.  A retained interval's midpoint can lie far
outside the displayed range.  For example, take $x_L=x_R=0$, $h_0=1$,
sensitivity $100\,\mathrm{cps}/(\mu\mathrm{Sv/h})$, an interval
$[0.5,100.5]$ with 100 counts, and an anchor point at $-10$ fixing the
left observation boundary.  Its midpoint pilot dose is initially
$0.01\,\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}$, giving $h=1$ and a contribution of
$0.15625\,\mathrm{cps}$ at zero.  Adding 100 point counts at midpoint
$50.5$ raises the pilot dose to $0.76\,\mu\mathrm{Sv/h}$ and shrinks
$h$ to approximately $0.13158$, making the interval contribution at zero
vanish.  Those extra counts lie outside the proposed $2h_0$ margin.

A safe single pilot-input hull for any candidate set is
$[\min(C)-h_0,\max(C)+h_0]$, but the union $U$ avoids unnecessary gaps.
Filtering affects computation only: an empty candidate set within a
nonempty measurement still receives the zero-count confidence limit
defined by the full observation extent.

\section{Summary of Complexity}
\label{sec:complexity}

Let $N$ be the number of point events, $L$ the number of original
intervals, $M\le2L$ the number of merged step events, and $Q$ the number
of queries.  For the fixed estimator, let $N_w$ and $M_w$ count entries
actually visited by the local accumulators, $J$ be the number of seeks
including numerical rebases, and $B_P$ be the work to allocate or extend
the cumulative step-weight cache.  The query cost is
\begin{equation}
  O\!\left(Q+N_w+M_w+J\log(N+1)+J\log(M+1)+B_P\right).
\end{equation}
The cache requires at most $O(M)$ initial work and storage; repeated
queries without data changes reuse its valid prefix.  Full-range sweeps
retain the original polynomial accumulation; narrow or disjoint ranges
skip unrelated entries.

For adaptive queries, let $S_c$ be the candidate count, $L_c$ its interval
portion, $V_I$ the number of interval-index nodes visited, $C_P$ the cost
of the fixed pilot pass at candidate centers, and $G_q$ the number of
active exact-bandwidth groups at final query $q$.  Let $\mathcal B$ contain
the queries inside observation-boundary strips and $\mathcal F$ the
queries requiring direct numerical fallback; $F_q$ counts the sources
reevaluated by that fallback.  With a prepared interval index, total cost is
\begin{equation}
  O\!\left(\log(N+1)+V_I+S_c\log(S_c+1)+C_P+Q
       +\sum_{q\in\mathcal B}G_q+\sum_{q\in\mathcal F}F_q\right).
\end{equation}
Each source generates a constant number of polynomial pieces and hence
$O(S_c)$ start/end events.  Sorting them costs $O(S_c\log(S_c+1))$; their
fixed-degree updates and the bounded periodic rebuilds cost $O(S_c)$.
Outer-boundary searches for every source and internal-boundary searches
for intervals cost $O(S_c\log(Q+1))$.  This is already absorbed by the
displayed bound, since
$S_c\log(Q+1)=O(S_c\log(S_c+1)+Q)$: for $Q>S_c$, use
$\log(Q/S_c)\le Q/S_c$; otherwise $\log(Q+1)\le\log(S_c+1)$.
The ordinary interior and exterior query paths cost $O(1)$ per query.
Thus their sweep is linear after boundary searches and sorting, independently of the
number of simultaneously overlapping sources.  This does not imply an
unconditional $O(S_c+Q)$ bound: boundary strips can contain many distinct
bandwidth groups, and fallback evaluates contributing sources directly.

The overlap search has the conservative bound
$V_I=O((L_c+1)\log(L+1))$.  Building the immutable interval index initially
costs $O(L\log(L+1))$ time and $O(L)$ space; it is reused across plot
queries until interval insertion, pruning, or clearing invalidates it.
Adaptive temporary storage is $O(S_c+Q)$ beyond these caches.  In the
worst case, boundary queries with one bandwidth per active source or
repeated fallback can still cost $O(S_cQ)$.

Snapshots share an already-built immutable interval index and copy the
valid step-weight prefix.  They do not build either cache on the source
estimator: repeated fresh snapshots of an uncached source still pay the
initial cache-building costs.  Copying the underlying data remains
$O(N+M+L)$; this optimization accelerates convolution and source selection,
not ingestion or full-dataset snapshot creation.
